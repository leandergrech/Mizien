# Flyovers, traffic, air pollution and noise: what has been shown?

**Claim Check 008 · Transport, Malta · Version 1.1 draft · 5 October 2026**

**Verdict: Not substantiated · Moderate confidence**

## The claim

Infrastructure Malta says that the Msida Creek flyover will reduce delays, emissions and noise, and that the completed Marsa Junction Project has reduced travel time and air pollution. The two statements refer to different projects and different evidence stages. This check assesses the published evidence for outcomes; it does not judge whether grade separation can improve a junction in principle.

## Summary

- **Marsa:** Infrastructure Malta reported 79% less travel time, up to 70% less particulate-matter emissions, 52% less NOx and 50% less CO2 in its April 2021 completion release. The release does not link the survey series or calculation behind the percentages.
- **There is a lead, not the underlying data:** in November 2021, Times of Malta quoted an Infrastructure Malta spokesperson saying periodic surveys confirmed time and air-quality benefits, despite traffic through the junction being over 19% higher than in 2013. The article did not publish those surveys or their methods.
- **Msida:** in October 2024, before works began, Infrastructure Malta presented the new flyover as a move to reduce delays, emissions and noise. The flyover opened to vehicles in December 2025, but the broader project remained in progress in 2026. The 2019 traffic observations in its project-description material are a planning baseline, not a post-opening comparison.
- **Noise is the least evidenced outcome:** no comparable before-and-after noise measurements were found for either site.
- **Verdict:** the claimed benefits are plausible, and Marsa has agency-reported figures. With no underlying surveys or local outcome series to inspect, and with Msida still under construction, the public evidence located does not independently substantiate the combined claim as stated.

## Wording and scope

Infrastructure Malta wrote on 28 October 2024 (wording as on the agency's page, checked 5 October 2026): “Msida has long been a traffic bottleneck, with congestion impacting air quality, noise levels, and overall accessibility. A cornerstone of the Msida Creek Project is the construction of a new flyover to replace the current traffic-light-controlled junction, a critical move to reduce delays, emissions, and noise pollution, making travel smoother and safer for all road users.” The same page says the project is “set for completion in 2027” and that initial work “will focus on strengthening the Msida waterfront quay”. Works had not begun when it was published, so the stated reductions could only be expected effects, not measured results.

For Marsa, the agency’s 15 April 2021 completion release said the project would cut travel time by 79% and air pollution by up to 70%. It separately stated 52% less nitrogen-oxide emissions and 50% less carbon-dioxide emissions, and said that eliminating traffic-light waiting times “is resulting in significant air quality improvements in Paola and Marsa”, a statement about local air quality for which it cites no monitoring data. Those are agency claims in a primary source. The underlying calculations are not supplied on that page.

The two sites therefore cannot be treated as one before-and-after test. Marsa is a completed multi-level junction with seven flyover structures and other works; the Msida Creek project includes road, canal, public-space and pedestrian elements and was incomplete at the review date.

## What the evidence shows

| Evidence | What it can establish | What it cannot establish |
|---|---|---|
| Infrastructure Malta Marsa completion release (2021) | The agency publicly reported large travel-time and emissions reductions. | The survey design, route/time coverage, baseline comparability, calculation method, or uncertainty. |
| Times of Malta interview (2021) | An IM spokesperson said periodic surveys confirmed benefits and reported traffic volume more than 19% above 2013. | Independent confirmation: the surveys and methods are not reproduced. |
| ERA Msida Creek planning record and 2023 project description | Planning-stage traffic observations from 2019 and forecast benefits. | Whether actual traffic, exposure or noise changed after the flyover opened. |
| Official Msida update (December 2025) | The flyover entered vehicle use; the project had more phases remaining. | A settled, whole-project before-and-after outcome. |
| Peer-reviewed US city study | Added road capacity can induce additional vehicle travel at network scale. | A local forecast for Marsa or Msida, or proof that either junction’s short-term travel-time gain did not occur. |

The Marsa figures refer to **emissions**, not necessarily measured ambient concentrations at nearby homes. Emissions estimates and local air-quality readings answer different questions. A strong evaluation would publish both, with the pollutant, locations, measurement periods and method specified.

## Induced travel and interpretation

Duranton and Turner’s study of US cities found that vehicle-kilometres travelled rose with interstate lane-kilometres. It is useful evidence that road capacity can change travel demand over time. It is not direct evidence about a single Maltese junction: city scale, road types, transit, population and network context differ. It therefore warns against assuming a one-off capacity increase guarantees lasting network-wide congestion relief, but it cannot disprove a local initial improvement.

The reported 19% increase in traffic at Marsa is also not enough by itself to establish induced demand. To test that mechanism, the project-level series would need to distinguish new trips from route shifts, time shifts, population and fleet changes, and growth in trips that would have occurred anyway.

## Fairness and verdict

Removing signal stops and separating crossing traffic can reduce delay for some movements. The public record contains a plausible mechanism and Infrastructure Malta’s reported estimates. This check does not claim the figures are false.

The gap is verification. The agency’s public release does not provide its Marsa survey or calculation; the later press account only relays the agency’s description. Msida’s public planning material gives forecasts and an old baseline, while construction was ongoing. No comparable ambient-noise measurements were found. On the evidence that could be inspected, the combined claim that flyovers cut congestion, noise and air pollution is **Not substantiated (moderate confidence)**.

This verdict does not mean the projects produced no benefit or that the reported percentages are wrong. It means the public material located does not let readers independently test them.

### Evidence that would resolve the open questions

1. Publish the Marsa before-and-after traffic survey files, route and time-window definitions, dates, counts, travel-time distributions, growth adjustment and uncertainty.
2. Publish the method and inputs behind each Marsa emissions percentage; separate emissions estimates from monitor readings of ambient concentration.
3. Publish post-opening Msida traffic, air-quality and noise measurements once the full road layout is operating, compared with a defined baseline and suitable control locations.
4. Report both junction-level outcomes and wider network outcomes over several years, accounting for traffic growth and route/time shifts.
