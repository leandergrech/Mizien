Fact ledger

A shared store of checked facts with sources, so a new claim is first tested against what we already know before anyone opens a PDF. This page sets out the design, what building it would cost, what it saves per claim, and what else it would change.

6 October 2026 · measured on the repository at 116 claims, 57 reports, 54 verdicts · hours are working hours (agent time plus maintainer review) unless marked

Saving per new check≈ 22%About 2.6 h on a 12 h check, rising as the store grows. Writing the analysis is not made faster.

The time saved is real but moderate. The stronger case is quality and upkeep: one checked value per fact across all reports, a list of the reports to revisit when Eurostat revises a series, and an archive gap of 460 source links made visible. Start with the lean phase, which pays back within the claims already in the queue, and decide on the rest once it has run for about 20 checks.

The ledger has to name the concept, scope and unit of every series.

The design

Facts stored as CSV in git are the source of truth. A Python build turns them into a SQLite file (standard library only) and JSON for the site. The graph is built from these tables at build time; there is no graph database. claim.yml stays the source of truth for claims and only gains fact IDs.

sourcesOne row per document: URL, archive copy, sha256, publisher, kind (primary, dataset, peer-reviewed, news), and whether it is second-hand. Merges sources.csv and the archive manifest.
series · observationsOne shared copy of each Eurostat, EEA or NSO table. The key is dataset, geography, item, unit and concept; each value records the release it came from and its flags.
factsF-0001… Kind (figure, target, finding, statement, document), value, unit, period, place or body, source and locator (e.g. "p. 114, Table 25"), grade A–D, checked by whom and when.
linksSub-claim → fact, as supports, contradicts or context. This is what turns separate reports into one body of evidence.
entitiesBodies, places and pledges (already registers), plus EU and national targets and named projects, so a claim about "the 2025 recycling target" finds every earlier check against it.
verbatimThe archived quoted wording, with source and locator, so a script can confirm every quotation in a report matches its source.

What happens when a claim arrives

  1. precheck.py reads the claim's wording and pulls out numbers, units, years, bodies and places, using the existing alias and place lists. An LLM may suggest matches, but each one is confirmed against the tables.
  2. It lists the series and targets that match, earlier sub-claims and their verdicts on the same body and topic, and any stored values a newer release has replaced.
  3. It writes a short brief to claims/CC-NNN/precheck.md: what we already hold, what is missing, and which sources still need archiving. Research starts from there.
  4. New facts go into the ledger with locators as the research finds them. A fact counts only once someone has checked it against the source page.
  5. CI fails when two reports disagree on a value, a quotation is not found in its archived source, or a figure in a report has no fact ID.

Time to build and consolidate

The estimate covers moving all 57 reports into the ledger: giving every check row an ID, linking the 197 sub-claims, merging the bibliographies, and loading shared copies of the 62 Eurostat tables and other datasets.

Why the saving grows: each new claim reuses more

For each report, in order, I counted how many of its datasets, source links and DOIs an earlier report had already used. Reuse is rising: in the latest batch (CC-109 to CC-115), more than half the datasets and over a quarter of the sources were already held. Today that overlap is fetched and checked again from scratch.

Saving over the number of claims

The model splits a check into parts and asks which parts a ledger shortens. Writing the analysis and report (about 4.5 h) is left unchanged. The finding phase gets shorter, reused data costs a lookup instead of a fetch, and some corrections are avoided. Each new check also costs extra time to enter its facts properly.

Impact beyond time

I read all 47 correction entries in the claims' research logs (31 of the 57 reports have at least one) and sorted them by cause. About half were the kind a ledger checks automatically: a figure out of scope or from an older release, a source dated wrongly or cited second-hand, a quotation that did not match the page.

  • One value per fact. Reports cannot drift apart on the same figure, and the denominator (which population, which emissions scope) is named once.
  • Re-check alerts. When Eurostat publishes a new inventory, the build lists every sub-claim resting on the revised series. Today nothing does this.
  • Archive coverage. 460 source links have no archive record. A single source table makes that list visible and gives a work queue for the about 230 that will need archiving by hand.
  • Faster right of reply and corrections. A body's reply that disputes a figure can be traced to every report that used it.
  • Readers. Each figure on a claim page can link to its source page and to the other checks that used it.

Order of work and decisions needed

  1. Lean phase (about 58 h). Rules and IDs, one source table, shared series copies, the conflict check, precheck.py. New checks write facts with IDs from then on.
  2. Review after about 20 checks. Compare the pre-check briefs with what the research found, and measure the time actually saved.
  3. Backfill (about 50 h). Link the 197 existing sub-claims to fact IDs, merge the bibliographies, add the quote check.
  4. Site. "Evidence used" on claim pages, series and target pages, computed link suggestions, retire the weak links.

Decisions for the maintainer:

  • Go ahead with the lean phase now, or the full build?
  • SQLite (recommended: needs nothing beyond Python) or DuckDB for the built database.
  • Whether to retire second- and third-degree links and demote Similar wording.
  • Whether to turn the 45,400 words of notes into finding rows now, or only for new claims.
  • Whether verdicts should ever be published as ClaimReview, and only after the right-of-reply deadline.
